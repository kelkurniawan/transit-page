## Why Correct Packing Matters So Much

Many cases of cargo damage in transit aren't caused by driver negligence or road conditions — they come down to **inadequate packing**. It's a fact shippers often overlook.

In land shipping, goods pass through a range of conditions:

- Shocks and vibration from uneven roads
- Pressure from other goods stacked on top
- Temperature swings (heat during the day when a truck is parked, cold at night)
- Lateral movement when a truck turns or brakes suddenly

Correct packing protects your goods against all of these at once, and makes loading and unloading easier too.

## The Basic Principles of Cargo Packing

Before getting into technique, understand three basic principles:

**1. Protect:** Goods need to be shielded from impact, pressure, and vibration.

**2. Contain:** The contents shouldn't be able to move inside the packaging. Empty space inside a box is the enemy — fill it with cushioning material.

**3. Inform:** Packaging needs clear labeling: destination, contents, handling instructions (fragile, this side up, do not stack, etc.).

## Choosing the Right Packing Materials

### For Fragile Items

**Bubble wrap** — the best protection for breakable items like ceramics, glass, and electronics. Wrap each item individually, and make sure no part is left uncovered.

**Foam packaging** — for electronics or precision instruments. Foam cut to match the shape of the item gives the best protection.

**Styrofoam** — suited to items with a specific shape, or bulk shipments that need lightweight but sturdy protection.

### For Heavy or Industrial Goods

**Wooden crates** — for heavy industrial goods like machinery, large components, or metal parts. Wooden crates provide strong structural protection and are easy to move with a forklift.

**Wooden pallets** — the industry standard for cargo shipping. They make forklift loading easier, protect goods from the ground and moisture, and simplify weight calculations.

### For General Goods

**Double-wall cardboard boxes** — for general cargo. Always use double-wall (not single-wall) cardboard for long-distance shipping like Jakarta-Bandung.

**Stretch wrap** — for wrapping pallets and keeping every item from shifting against the others.

## Step-by-Step Packing Instructions

### Step 1: Choose the Right Box Size

A box that's too big means too much empty space, which means goods shift and knock against each other.
A box that's too small means goods get compressed and damage the packaging.

Ideally, leave 2–3 cm of space on every side between the item and the box wall to fill with cushioning material.

### Step 2: Protect Each Item Individually

For every item going into a single box, protect it individually before placing it inside. This keeps items from banging into each other.

### Step 3: Fill Empty Space

Use bubble wrap, crumpled newspaper, air pillows, or foam offcuts to fill empty space inside the box. **Shake the box once it's packed** — if you can still hear things shifting, add more filler material.

### Step 4: Seal and Reinforce With Tape

Seal every seam of the box with **packing tape (heavy-duty brown tape)**, not ordinary scotch tape. Reinforce every fold, especially the bottom, which bears the load. Tape in an "H" or "I" pattern to make sure nothing is left unsealed.

### Step 5: Arrange on a Pallet (If Needed)

For bulk shipments:

- Place heavier items at the bottom, lighter items on top
- Stack neatly and stably — nothing should overhang the edge of the pallet
- Wrap the whole stack with at least 3–4 layers of stretch film
- Make sure the pallet doesn't wobble when moved

### Step 6: Clear Labeling

This step gets overlooked often but matters a great deal. Every package needs:

- **Destination label:** Recipient's name, full address (including house number, RT/RW, postal code), recipient's phone number
- **Origin label:** Sender's name and contact
- **Handling label:** FRAGILE, THIS SIDE UP, DO NOT STACK (as needed)
- **Content label:** A general description of the contents (useful if an incident occurs)

Print labels clearly and place them somewhere easy to see (the largest side of the box). On brown cardboard, red or yellow labels stand out best.

## The Most Common Packing Mistakes

### Using Weakened, Previously-Used Boxes

A box that's already been used has reduced structural strength. For long-distance cargo shipping, always use new boxes, or ones still in prime condition.

### Overpacking a Single Box

Overloading makes a box bulge and weakens its walls. It's better to use two boxes than one overloaded box.

### Skipping Corner Protection

The corners of a box are its most vulnerable points. Use corner protectors made of heavy cardboard or foam for valuable items.

### Relying on a Single Layer of Bubble Wrap for Very Fragile Items

A single layer of bubble wrap isn't enough for highly breakable items. Use at least 2–3 layers, and make sure the entire surface is covered.

## Special Markings for Specific Types of Goods

**Electronics:** Add "FRAGILE - ELECTRONICS" and "KEEP AWAY FROM WATER" markings

**Tube- or pipe-shaped items:** Make sure both ends are protected with dedicated caps or wood

**Liquids:** Make sure the seal is watertight, then place inside a sealed plastic bag before boxing. Mark "LIQUID - DO NOT INVERT"

**Important documents:** Place inside a sealed plastic bag and keep somewhere easily accessible, not at the bottom of a stack

## The Role of Packing in Insurance Claims

If damage occurs and you file a claim, the condition of the packaging is the primary piece of evidence. Shipments packed carelessly run the risk of having claims denied, since the damage may be attributed to inadequate packaging rather than negligence on the freight company's part.

Document the condition of your goods before shipping (photos before and after packing) as supporting evidence for a claim, should you ever need one.
